More and more people now ask a chatbot instead of typing a query into a search engine. That means a growing share of what you publish is read first by a machine, which then decides whether to repeat, summarise or cite you. These are my notes from the newsletter *Artificial Ignorance* and a few related pieces on what that shift means for anyone who writes.

## Who is reading your writing now?

The starting point of the piece is simple: AI is becoming one of the main readers of what we publish. Your blog post, report or essay may end up in the **training data** of a large language model (LLM), the huge collection of text a model learns from before anyone talks to it. It may also be fetched live when an AI search tool looks for sources to answer a question.

So the practical question becomes: what makes an AI system pick up your work and cite it?

## What makes content get cited by AI systems?

The tactical advice, according to the piece, mostly boils down to doing good work and making it findable. It lists four things:

| Advice | What it means in practice |
|---|---|
| Write good content | AI models and search algorithms both favour high-quality information. |
| Follow E-E-A-T | Experience, Expertise, Authoritativeness and Trustworthiness: the quality signals Google has long promoted for search. Show who you are and why you know this. |
| Care about user experience | Well-organised, easy to navigate, quick to load. |
| Earn backlinks and citations | Links and references to your work from reputable sources. |

If this sounds like ordinary search engine optimisation (SEO), that is the point. The basics did not change much. What changed is who is doing the reading.

## The three ambitions of "writing for the AIs"

The more interesting part of the piece is philosophical. The author breaks "writing for the AIs" into three escalating ambitions:

1. **Teaching AIs what you know.** Useful, but time-limited: models will eventually learn to do much of this better themselves.
2. **Persuading AIs of your beliefs.** Likely futile, because any single essay is a drop in the ocean of training data.
3. **Helping AIs simulate you.** Technically possible, but existentially uncomfortable. Do you want a model that writes like you after you stop writing?

The author also questions a shared assumption behind framings like writing as influence, writing as persuasion and writing as preservation. All three assume that the value of writing is defined by what happens to it *after* you publish. He is not sure he accepts that.

His conclusion is that the value is not in volume. It is in writing things that literally do not exist on the internet yet.

## "Be generous in what you write and selfish in what you choose to write about"

This was the line that stayed with me. The author paraphrases it as his operating principle, one he arrived at more or less by accident, and credits it as the main reason he has kept writing for so long.

- **Selfish** in choosing topics: write about things you genuinely want to understand, so your own curiosity comes first.
- **Generous** in the writing: make the exploration public, so others can follow and use it.

The related idea is **writing to think**. If you write to work something out for yourself, the audience, human or machine, is secondary. The writing has already done its job by the time anyone reads it.

## What is GEO (Generative Engine Optimization)?

A related field is growing under the name **GEO, Generative Engine Optimization**: the practice of making content more likely to show up in answers produced by generative AI tools, such as chatbots and AI search, rather than only in a traditional list of search results.

One widely shared prediction from Gartner was that search engine volume would drop by 25% by 2026, because of AI chatbots and other virtual agents. That was a forecast, not a measurement, so it is worth checking against real data rather than repeating as fact.

The GEO advice I collected starts from the idea that **your reputation precedes you**. When AI models generate responses, they draw on patterns observed across their training data. A source that is consistently described as reliable is more likely to be echoed. Common recommendations are:

- Integrate relevant statistics and quantitative data.
- Include expert quotes and testimonials.
- Create dedicated FAQ sections that directly answer common questions.
- Cite authoritative external sources.

A further step is to look at how AI search tools currently describe you or your organisation, so you can see the gap and shape your own narrative.

A small example: if you write a guide to a research method, an FAQ heading like "How many interviews do I need for a qualitative study?" mirrors the way people phrase questions to a chatbot. That makes it easier for an AI tool to match your answer to the question.

## A question I keep asking myself

Reading these two pieces side by side, I notice a tension. GEO advice tells you to shape your writing so that machines pick it up. The "writing to think" view tells you that the reader, machine or human, is secondary.

As a sociologist, I also wonder who decides which sources count as "authoritative" once an AI system is doing the choosing, and whose voices get left out of that judgement.

So the question I am holding on to, rather than an answer: **can you write for your own curiosity first and still be findable, or does optimising for AI readers slowly change what you choose to write about?**

## Key takeaways

- Getting cited by AI systems mostly comes down to good, well-organised, trustworthy content that others link to and cite.
- "Writing for the AIs" can mean teaching them, persuading them or helping them simulate you. Each has clear limits.
- The lasting value is in writing what does not exist online yet, and in writing to think.
- GEO adds concrete tactics: statistics, expert quotes, FAQ sections and cited sources.
- Gartner's prediction of a 25% drop in search engine volume by 2026 is a forecast. Treat it as one.
