One of the easiest ways to understand where AI is actually going is to look at what young companies are betting on. Startups have to find a real problem someone will pay to solve, so their pitches are a rough map of where AI meets everyday work. These are my notes from three sources: a review of 139 Y Combinator (YC) startups, a TechCrunch list of 16 top YC ventures, and the *AI Valley* newsletter.

**Y Combinator** is a well-known startup accelerator in the US. It funds batches of early-stage companies and helps them launch, so its batches are often read as a signal of what founders think is promising.

## What did 139 YC startups have in common?

The review grouped the companies into a few big categories.

### AI Ops: making models work in real businesses

**AI Ops** here means tooling and platforms that help companies deploy AI models that actually work. The category had become very large, and it was mostly about taking large language models (LLMs) and taming them for production use, meaning real, everyday business tasks rather than demos.

The pain points these startups targeted included:

- Solving customer support tickets
- Negotiating sales contracts
- Writing drafts of legal documents
- Building no-code LLM workflows (setting up AI processes without writing code)
- On-premise LLM deployment (running the model on a company's own servers)
- Automating trust and safety moderation (reviewing harmful content on platforms)

### Developer tools

Apps, plugins and SDKs (software development kits, ready-made building blocks for programmers) that make it easier to write code. Common examples: integrating third-party data, auto-generating code and tests, and working with agents or chatbots to build and debug code.

### Healthcare and biotech

Healthcare seems to have a lot of room for automation. Startups were working on note-taking, billing, training and prescribing. On the biotech side, some were building autonomous surgery robots and at-home cancer detection.

The review made a point I found important: **large enterprises, healthcare providers and governments are not going to send sensitive data to an outside AI provider such as OpenAI.** That leaves a gap for startups that build on-premise, compliant LLMs for these sectors. "Compliant" means the system follows the data protection and industry rules that apply, for example around patient records.

This is also a question of trust and power: who holds the data, and who is allowed to see it, shapes which AI products can exist in a sector at all.

### Finance and payments

Startups targeting banks, fintechs and compliance departments. The range was wide, from automated debt collection to AI due diligence (checking a company before investing in it) to a "Copilot for bankers".

## 16 top YC ventures (from TechCrunch)

TechCrunch picked out 16 YC ventures. Here they are with a one-line description of each:

| # | Company | What it does |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | ARC Prize Foundation | Benchmarks and competitions to measure progress towards AGI |
| 2 | Asimov | Collects videos of human movement to train humanoid robots |
| 3 | Avoice | Automates non-design paperwork for architecture firms |
| 4 | Button Computer | AI wearable that controls apps by voice |
| 5 | CodeWisp | Creates games directly from natural-language descriptions |
| 6 | Crosslayer Labs | Detects and prevents website impersonation and phishing scams |
| 7 | Doomersion | Immersive language learning inside a short-video feed |
| 8 | Lexius | Builds advanced AI into security camera systems |
| 9 | Librar Labs | AI library management for schools (collections, cataloguing) |
| 10 | Milliray | Radar systems that track small drones |
| 11 | MouseCat | AI that investigates and identifies fraud |
| 12 | Opalite Health | AI translation in healthcare, helping staff talk with patients who don't speak English |
| 13 | Sequence Markets | One-stop trading platform (crypto, prediction markets and more) |
| 14 | ShoFo | Global video library and index that AI labs can use for datasets |
| 15 | Sonarly | AI that finds the root cause of software production problems and tries to fix them |
| 16 | Terranox AI | AI exploration for uranium in North America, for next-generation nuclear power |

**AGI** stands for artificial general intelligence, a hypothetical AI that can do most intellectual tasks a person can.

A pattern worth noticing: several of these sell **data** rather than models (Asimov, ShoFo), and several move AI into physical or safety-critical settings (drones, cameras, hospitals, uranium exploration).

## What AI Valley highlighted

Two items from the *AI Valley* newsletter:

- **OpenAI is building a "superapp".** By combining browsing, coding and chat in one environment, the system can work across tasks instead of only replying. It can access context, write code and take actions in the same place, so users jump between tools less.
- **Google upgraded AI Studio with a full-stack coding agent.** An agent is an AI system that can plan and take steps on its own, not just answer. "Full-stack" means it can work on both the visible part of an app and the server side behind it.

## Key takeaways

- A large share of AI startups work on AI Ops: making LLMs reliable enough for real business tasks.
- Developer tools, healthcare and biotech, and finance were other big clusters.
- Sensitive sectors such as healthcare, government and large enterprises create demand for on-premise, compliant AI.
- Many new ventures focus on data supply or on bringing AI into physical, high-stakes settings.
- The big platforms are moving towards all-in-one environments where AI can browse, code and act.
