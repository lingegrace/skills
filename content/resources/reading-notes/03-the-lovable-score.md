When AI makes it easy to copy any feature in weeks, what keeps people using your product? Elena Verna's answer, in her newsletter *Elena's Growth Scoop*, is that growth is becoming a trust problem, and that you need a way to measure whether people genuinely love what you have built. These are my notes on the "Lovable Score" she uses for Lovable, an AI tool for building apps and websites from plain-language prompts.

## Why are features no longer enough?

Verna's argument starts with a blunt observation: **feature differentiation is collapsing.** If a competitor can ship the same feature quickly, a feature alone stops being a reason to choose you.

What is left is trust and affection. In her words, growth is becoming a trust problem, and the products making the biggest splash are the ones people genuinely love.

From a sociological angle, this is a shift from competing on *what a product does* to competing on *the relationship people have with it*. Relationships are harder to copy than features.

## North Star metric vs Lovable Score

Most growth teams track a **North Star metric**: one number that best captures whether the business is moving, such as weekly active users or projects created. It is quantitative and tells you about behaviour.

Verna adds a second, qualitative measure built from user surveys. Her framing:

| | What it tells you | Type |
|---|---|---|
| North Star metric | Is the business moving? | Quantitative (behaviour) |
| Lovable Score | Do users still care? | Qualitative composite (feelings, from surveys) |

She calls the pair "BFFs": quant North Star plus qual composite score. You need both. A product can grow for a while on habit or lack of alternatives, even while users quietly stop liking it. The Lovable Score is meant to catch that early.

## What goes into the Lovable Score?

The score combines four types of survey measure, weighted and converted to a single 100-point scale. The focus is on **NPS** (willingness to recommend) and **PMF** (how irreplaceable the product is). Here are the four, with the targets and Verna's own reactions to hitting them:

| Measure | Survey question | Target | Verna's reaction |
|---|---|---|---|
| **NPS** (Net Promoter Score) | How likely are you to recommend us? | 60+ | Happy |
| ↳ Bonus: actual referrals | "Did you actually refer Lovable to a friend or family member in the last 30 days?" | 70%+ say yes | Very happy |
| **PMF score** (Product-Market Fit) | "How would you feel if you could no longer use Lovable?" | 60% "very disappointed" | Very, very happy |
| **CSAT** (Customer Satisfaction) | "Overall, how satisfied are you with Lovable?" (1–7) | 70% give 7, "very satisfied" | Happy |
| **CES** (Customer Effort Score) | "How easy was it to accomplish what you came to Lovable to do?" (1–7) | 50% give 7, "very easy" | Solid |

A few terms explained:

- **NPS** asks people to rate, usually from 0 to 10, how likely they are to recommend a product. The score is the share of enthusiastic "promoters" minus the share of unhappy "detractors", so it can range from −100 to +100. A score of 60 or more is very high.
- **The bonus question** checks behaviour, not intention. Saying you would recommend something is easy. Having actually told a friend in the last 30 days is stronger evidence.
- **PMF score** comes from the classic product-market fit survey question: how disappointed would you be if you could no longer use this?
- **CSAT** and **CES** measure satisfaction and ease. Note that Verna counts only the top box (a 7 out of 7), a deliberately strict bar.

## The four questions behind the score

Strip away the acronyms and the Lovable Score asks four plain questions:

1. **Are we easy to use?** (CES)
2. **Are we creating happy users?** (CSAT)
3. **Are we irreplaceable?** (PMF)
4. **Do people actually want to talk about us?** (NPS and real referrals)

These are useful even if you never build a formal score.

## How could a team adapt it?

The following are my suggestions, not part of Verna's framework.

- **Start with the four questions.** A small team or a student project can run a five-question survey with a free form tool and look at the answers before worrying about weights.
- **Set your own targets.** Lovable's targets reflect its product and users. Run the survey once to get a baseline, then decide what "happy" looks like for you.
- **Decide the weights openly.** Verna's notes say the measures are weighted, but whether you weight NPS or ease of use more heavily is a choice about what you value. Write that choice down.
- **Track it next to one behavioural number.** Pair the score with a North Star metric so that feelings and behaviour can check each other.
- **Ask who is answering.** Survey results reflect the people who chose to respond. Heavy users are often over-represented, and people who left may be missing entirely.
- **For internal tools, including AI skills:** the same four questions work for a tool your colleagues use. Is it easy? Are they satisfied? Would they miss it? Have they told anyone about it?

## Key takeaways

- When AI makes features easy to copy, being loved and trusted becomes a key source of growth.
- A North Star metric shows whether the business is moving. The Lovable Score shows whether users still care.
- The score combines NPS (plus a real-referral check), PMF, CSAT and CES into a 100-point composite.
- Lovable's targets are strict: NPS 60+, 70%+ referring in the last 30 days, 60% "very disappointed", 70% top-box satisfaction, 50% top-box ease.
- The four plain questions (easy, happy, irreplaceable, talked about) are usable by any team.
