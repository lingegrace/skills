Discussions of global AI rules often mention China, but the original documents are long and mostly in Chinese. This article summarises the key ones so you can see what each says, in its own terms. Every position below is attributed to the document that states it. It is a summary, not an assessment.

## What does the 2017 national AI plan set out?

In July 2017 China's State Council, the country's cabinet, issued the **New Generation Artificial Intelligence Development Plan** (document Guofa [2017] No. 35), addressed to provincial governments and central ministries. It sets a three-step strategy.

| Step | Overall goal | AI core industry | Related industries |
|---|---|---|---|
| **By 2020** | AI technology and applications keep pace with the world's advanced level; AI becomes a new driver of growth | Over 150 billion yuan | Over 1 trillion yuan |
| **By 2025** | Major breakthroughs in basic theory; some technologies and applications world-leading; AI a main driver of industrial upgrading | Over 400 billion yuan | Over 5 trillion yuan |
| **By 2030** | AI theory, technology and applications at a world-leading level overall; China a major global AI innovation centre | Over 1 trillion yuan | Over 10 trillion yuan |

"Core industry" means AI products and services themselves. "Related industries" are the wider sectors AI is expected to drive.

The plan also links each step to rules. By 2020, initial ethical norms and policies in some areas. By 2025, initial laws, ethical norms and a policy system, plus AI safety assessment and control capabilities. By 2030, a more complete legal, ethical and policy system.

### The four pillars

The plan's technical agenda can be read as four building blocks:

1. **Basic theory:** the fundamental scientific questions, the "why".
2. **Key common technologies:** reusable technical modules that turn theory into tools. Examples include knowledge computing engines, cross-media analysis, swarm intelligence, hybrid human–machine intelligence, autonomous unmanned systems, virtual-reality modelling, AI chips and natural language processing.
3. **Support platforms:** shared platforms, data, tools and testing resources that make development and deployment efficient.
4. **Intelligent infrastructure:** the network, data and computing infrastructure that AI applications run on.

### The eight areas of basic theory

- **Big data intelligence:** using massive data together with human knowledge for learning and reasoning.
- **Cross-media perception and computing:** understanding images, sound and text together, as people do.
- **Hybrid augmented intelligence:** humans and AI working together rather than AI replacing people.
- **Swarm intelligence:** many simple agents, such as drones or robots, cooperating like a flock of birds.
- **Autonomous coordinated control and optimised decision-making:** unmanned systems, such as self-driving cars, deciding and coordinating in complex environments.
- **Advanced machine learning:** methods that are more efficient and robust, need less data and protect privacy.
- **Brain-inspired intelligent computing:** computing models modelled on the brain's structure and function.
- **Quantum intelligent computing:** using quantum effects for major gains in AI computing power.

## What has China's Ministry of Foreign Affairs published?

The Ministry of Foreign Affairs (MFA) maintains a page of documents on AI. (Domestic internet and AI rules are mainly the responsibility of the Cyberspace Administration of China and are outside the scope of this summary.)

### Global AI Governance Initiative (2023)

According to MFA speeches, President Xi Jinping put forward the **Global AI Governance Initiative** in 2023. In an October 2025 speech at an AI capacity-building workshop, the MFA's coordinator for AI affairs, Sun Xiaobo, summarised the approach as three commitments: innovation and openness, fairness and inclusiveness (making AI accessible), and joint governance, with the UN as the main channel. He also said AI "should not become a game for rich countries and rich people".

### Position paper on strengthening AI ethics governance (updated January 2024)

The paper starts from the concern that misuse of AI could harm human dignity and equality, infringe human rights, deepen discrimination and bias, and challenge legal systems. It addresses governments in four areas:

| Area | Main points |
|---|---|
| **Regulation** | Put ethics first; build ethical guidelines, accountability and ethics review; use risk early warning and "agile governance" with tiered, category-based management; build ethical systems suited to each country's conditions. |
| **Research and development** | Developers should self-regulate, avoid immature technologies with serious negative consequences, keep AI under human control, make algorithms transparent, explainable and traceable, improve data quality and review data and algorithms for bias. |
| **Use** | Prohibit AI uses that violate laws and ethics; assess products before use; protect privacy and allow consent to be withdrawn; support public AI ethics education. |
| **International cooperation** | Encourage cross-border exchange and shared benefits; oppose exclusive blocs and obstruction of other countries' technological development; call for an international agreement on AI ethics based on universal participation. |

### Position on military uses of AI

China submitted this paper under UN General Assembly resolution 79/239 (2024). It describes a "people-centred military AI" approach and refers to China's 2021 position paper to the Convention on Certain Conventional Weapons. It states five principles:

1. **Prudence and responsibility:** major powers should not seek absolute military advantage through AI and should prevent arms races.
2. **People-centred:** humans remain ultimately responsible, and humans control relevant weapon systems.
3. **AI for good:** military AI should support peace and comply with international humanitarian law.
4. **Agile governance:** forward-looking risk assessment and measures to reduce proliferation risk.
5. **Multilateralism:** support the UN's role and a governance framework with broad participation.

It adds three suggestions: set guiding principles, improve governance measures such as risk testing systems, and strengthen international cooperation.

### AI Capacity-Building Action Plan for Good and for All

This plan aims to bridge digital and AI divides, especially for the Global South. It commits to implementing UN resolution **A/RES/78/311** on AI capacity-building through North–South, South–South and triangular cooperation. According to the MFA, China and Zambia launched a Group of Friends for International Cooperation on AI Capacity-building as a follow-up, and China has hosted capacity-building workshops in Shanghai and Beijing.

### Global AI Governance Action Plan (2025)

Released in July 2025 at the World AI Conference and High-Level Meeting on Global AI Governance in Shanghai, where China also proposed a World AI Cooperation Organization. It ties its aims to the UN Pact for the Future and Global Digital Compact. Its 13 points:

1. Seize AI opportunities together.
2. Promote AI innovation, including open "AI+" application scenarios.
3. Apply AI across industries: manufacturing, health, education, agriculture, poverty reduction.
4. Speed up digital infrastructure: clean power, networks, computing, data centres, common compute standards.
5. Build an open, diverse innovation ecosystem, including cross-border open-source communities.
6. Supply high-quality data, with lawful data flows, privacy and diverse datasets.
7. Address energy and environment through "sustainable AI" and green computing.
8. Build consensus on standards through bodies such as the ITU, ISO and IEC.
9. Let the public sector lead in deploying reliable AI in health, education and transport.
10. Govern AI safety: risk testing, tiered management, threat sharing, traceability.
11. Implement the Global Digital Compact, with the UN as the main channel.
12. Put international cooperation on AI capacity-building at the centre, including women's and children's digital rights.
13. Build inclusive, multi-stakeholder governance.

The MFA page also lists the Shanghai Declaration on Global AI Governance and the "AI+" International Cooperation Initiative.

## Which UN General Assembly resolutions are referenced?

- **78/265** (21 March 2024): seizing the opportunities of safe, secure and trustworthy AI systems for sustainable development; covers non-military AI and calls for risk assessment, transparency, human oversight, content authentication and closing digital divides.
- **78/311**: enhancing international cooperation on AI capacity-building; recalls the four resolutions below and focuses on non-military uses.
- **78/213** (19 December 2023): human rights in the context of digital technologies; calls for human rights impact assessments, transparency and protection from algorithmic discrimination and unlawful surveillance.
- **78/132** (19 December 2023): information and communications technologies for sustainable development; focuses on access gaps, including for women and girls and the least developed countries.
- **77/320** (25 July 2023): the impact of rapid technological change on achieving the Sustainable Development Goals.
- **79/239** (2024): the basis for China's submission on military AI.

## What to watch

- How the proposed World AI Cooperation Organization takes shape.
- Progress on the 2025 and 2030 goals of the 2017 plan, and any successor plan.
- How the Global Digital Compact and UN processes such as the Global Dialogue on AI Governance and the international scientific panel on AI develop.
- Implementation of resolution 78/311 and related capacity-building programmes.
- How positions on military AI evolve in UN discussions.

## Key takeaways

- The 2017 State Council plan sets three steps (2020, 2025, 2030) with core AI industry targets of 150 billion, 400 billion and 1 trillion yuan.
- Its technical agenda covers basic theory, key common technologies, support platforms and intelligent infrastructure.
- MFA documents stress UN-centred, inclusive governance, ethics in four areas, and five principles for military AI.
- The 2025 Global AI Governance Action Plan sets out 13 points, from infrastructure to capacity-building.
- These documents build on UN resolutions 78/265, 78/311, 78/213, 78/132 and 77/320.
